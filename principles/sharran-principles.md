# Sharran Srivatsaa: First Principles

Recurring, load-bearing beliefs behind his advice. Each is evidenced in two or more corpus docs (see sharran-sources.md).

1. **Time is the business partner, speed is not.** He flipped "a hundred homes" in five years while a friend went from one property to a fourplex to 20 units, and "his net worth was five times that of mine because I was working on speed and he was working on time." He moved his own fund from a 5 year horizon that "barely beat the S&P 500" to a 100 year horizon and had "our 10 best years ever."

2. **When money comes in, the first question is "what can I own with this?"** Not what to spend it on. Zero people made the Forbes 400 on earned income; the vast majority are "buyers and builders." Messi cut base salary roughly 96% for revenue share and equity, because "the most valuable asset on earth isn't being paid for the work that you do. It's being compensated for the value that you help to create."

3. **Borrow against assets, do not sell them.** "The IRS clearly states that borrowed money is not income." A $500,000 line of credit is "zero federal income tax and zero capital gains tax," and the portfolio keeps compounding while you spend the proceeds. Musk borrowed against Tesla stock to buy Twitter. The goal is access to capital, not a pile of money.

4. **It is not what you make, it is what you keep.** After taxes and fees, "only about 50-60% of what you make is actually the return you keep." So he optimizes the drag: S corp elections, opportunity zones, depreciation, step-up in basis. "If you can defer your taxes, you actually avoided your taxes."

5. **Structure before returns.** "The money that you make should never go to you personally first. It should go into a trust." A living trust runs roughly $3,000 once, and "just one lawsuit or one bad situation, and it pays for itself a hundred times over." Vanderbilts hit zero millionaire descendants; Rockefellers still support 250-plus heirs, a difference he attributes to structure, not luck.

6. **Every dollar needs a job.** Idle cash is lazy cash. His coaches asked him to imagine "all 100 of your employees were taking a nap at their desks," then: "your retirement is sitting in your checking account funding someone else's retirement." His routing account, the quarterback, "never holds money."

7. **Never risk the empire.** "You don't risk the empire for a pot of gold." A friend put $700,000 of savings into one oil and gas deal and lost it all; the lesson "is about sizing the bet." And: "if you hear asymmetric upside and think go all in, that's how people blow up. The goal isn't to win one big time. The goal is to never lose the game."

8. **Concentrate only where you understand the risk and have control.** Diversification "is only a hedge for what you don't understand or control." Operators like Musk and Bezos hold everything in their own companies because they own the insider information and the control. The real risk is never price movement, it is "not understanding what you actually own."

9. **Score it, do not guess it.** He froze at the screen until he "stopped guessing and started using a system." The four goods run in order: good people, good intentions, good rationale, and only then good contracts. The investment x-ray scores capital preservation, tax efficiency, cash flow and growth at 25 points each.

10. **Every expensive mistake must buy a durable principle.** A $1 million loss to a founder who vanished produced the four goods: "I needed to document my learning so this never happened again." If a mistake left behind no checklist, framework, story or quote, he says you did not actually learn from it.

11. **Effort is the one lever you fully control, and environment sets its level.** His father: "it doesn't matter how good you are. You have to work like everything is against you." Unreasonable effort is "doing something so many times that the rest of the world thinks that you're crazy for doing it that way." At Goldman, superiority plus "crippling insecurity" produced work ethic "you cannot manufacture on your own."

12. **If you cannot solve a problem, it is because you are playing by the rules.** The bestseller list was "rigged," so they bypassed it entirely and took a Guinness World Record instead. Paramount bypassed the board and went straight to shareholders. Question the frame, not the solution inside it.

13. **Build the game plan before you go looking.** "The most important thing to do before you start searching for homes is to build a game plan." Jorge Mas began preparing for Messi two years before free agency. The visible result rests on unseen preparation: "if you don't prepare, you just don't care."

## What he refuses

- "You don't risk the empire for a pot of gold. You don't risk 15-plus years of savings all on one deal."
- "I never pay my children for chores" because "it creates a negative association with work."
- Selling to raise cash when the asset can be collateral instead. "You lived off that loan for years without ever selling your stock or paying taxes on it."
- Speculative collateral. Banks "generally will not lend against" meme coins or penny stocks "because they can't be underwritten."
- Timing the market. Ten days hold half the market's total returns, and the investor who sat in cash got the worst return of the three. Do not touch investments "for 90 days after a 20% market drop."
- Handing a child a pretend project instead of something they actually own.
- Selling out of fear: "definitely not when you're scared, definitely not when the price drops."
- Letting lifestyle grow with income. Lifestyle creep is "the number one reason most high earners never reach financial freedom." He and his wife lived off one income for 14 years.
- Passive income with nobody active underneath it. "If it's passive for you, it has to be active for someone else." A "daisy chain of people doing passive things" is the scam pattern.

## Voice

He opens by earning attention, never with detail: the three whys, "why is this important, why is this important right now, and why should you listen to me." He numbers everything: 12 steps, four stages, five D's, seven laws, three zones, five milestones, four goods.

He names his frameworks and reuses the names: the quarterback, the four goods, the investment x-ray, deal DNA, lazy cash, the Ellison squeeze, the forever five-year double.

He teaches by pairing one story against one number. Vanderbilt against Rockefeller. His flipping against his friend's holding. He always gives the exact figure: $106 million in 72 hours, 39 interviews, 19.9% over 60 years.

He hedges his own math openly, calling the x-ray score "arbitrary," his portfolio "one example, not a universal recommendation," and the five-year double a demonstration "rather than a guaranteed return." He opens worked examples with "let's say," addresses the viewer as "you," and closes sections with questions rather than a conclusion.
